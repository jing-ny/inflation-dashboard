# March 2026 Update: Q1 Looked Simpler in January

**Q1 started as a simple disinflation story. It did not end that way.**

---

## The Big Picture

Global disinflation was working. Then a war broke out.

Through January, the story was clean: US inflation fell to 2.4%, the Euro Area hit 1.7%, Japan slipped below the BoJ's 2% target for the first time since 2022. Central banks were vindicated. The hard landing never came.

Then the Iran war erupted in March. Oil spiked to $120/bbl. The Strait of Hormuz — 20% of global oil and LNG supply — was disrupted. And every inflation forecast written before mid-March became a rough draft.

**Q2 will test whether March was a temporary shock, or the start of a harder inflation path.**

## Three Stories That Define Q1

### 1. Australia: The Outlier That Hiked

Everyone else held or stood pat. The RBA raised rates.

Australian trimmed mean inflation hit 3.4% in the December quarter — substantially above the 2-3% target band and well above the RBA's own forecast. The Board hiked 25bp to 3.85% in February, projecting inflation won't return to the target band until early 2027.

**Why it matters:** Australia is now the only major economy tightening. With headline CPI at 3.8% in January — the highest among advanced economies — the RBA may not be done.

### 2. The Canada Surprise

Canada's CPI plunged from 2.3% in January to **1.8% in February** — the sharpest one-month drop in Q1. The cause? Base effects from the GST/HST tax holiday that ended in Feb 2025. The BoC, already at 2.25% after an aggressive cutting cycle, now faces a question: is this *too much* disinflation?

**Watch for:** Whether the tariff uncertainty (US trade review) creates a second-half inflation rebound that the BoC can't ignore.

### 3. China Wakes Up

After months of deflation, China's CPI jumped to **1.3% in February** — from just 0.2% in January. The culprit: Lunar New Year seasonal effects. But it's the first convincingly positive print in over a year. The PBoC has held the 1Y LPR at 3.00% for 10 consecutive months, and the IMF still projects only 0.7% for the full year.

**The real question:** Is this a seasonal blip or the first sign that Beijing's stimulus is finally reaching consumers?

---

## The War Premium

The Iran conflict reshaped the forecast landscape in a single week. Here's how central bank projections shifted *after* the first shots:

- **ECB March projections:** 2026 HICP revised **1.9% → 2.6%** (+0.7pp) — the largest single-quarter revision in ECB history. Almost entirely energy-driven.
- **Fed March SEP:** PCE inflation revised **2.5% → 2.7%** — modest, reflecting the US energy independence advantage.
- **RBA:** Already dealing with domestic inflation overshoot; energy adds fuel to an existing fire.

Oil settled around $90/bbl after Trump declared operations "very complete." But the Hormuz shipping disruption lingers, and LNG prices remain elevated. The divergence between CB forecasts (which price in the war) and IMF projections (pre-war) tells the story: for the ECB, it's a 0.7pp gap. For the Fed, just 0.3pp. Energy importers are exposed; producers are buffered.

## What to Watch in Q2

1. **IMF April WEO** — First post-conflict global forecast. Expect meaningful upward revisions to 2026 inflation, especially for energy importers.
2. **UK/AU February CPI** (Mar 25) — Both releasing tomorrow. If Australia prints above 4%, the RBA hiking cycle could extend.
3. **Oil prices** — If Hormuz disruption persists, $100+ oil reprices everything. If it resolves, Q1 becomes a volatility footnote.
4. **Fed June FOMC** — With PCE at 2.7% projected, does the Fed cut at all in 2026? The dot plot says one cut. Markets are skeptical.
5. **BoE May MPR** — UK inflation fell to 3.0% but remains the highest in the G7. The 5-4 vote split in February suggests a cut is coming — unless energy prices intervene.
