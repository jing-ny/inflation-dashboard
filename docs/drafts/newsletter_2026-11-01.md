# Inflation Newsletter — 2026-11-01

Four of the five largest advanced-economy prints moved up together this period, and none of them moved a little. The Euro Area, Australia, the UK and the US all posted material increases in their latest readings — a synchronised step-up that leaves several central banks visibly behind their own published projections.

The Euro Area led it: September CPI at 3.75%, up 0.52pp from 3.23% in August. That is the largest single move in the panel, and it sits awkwardly against the ECB's September 2026 forecast of 3.0% for the year, with the deposit rate held at 2.00%. The ECB's own note flags an upward revision to 2.6% on Middle East energy prices; the realised print is running ahead of even that.

Australia's August reading rose to 4.0% from 3.5% in July, +0.5pp — the highest headline rate in the panel outside South Africa. The RBA has already hiked twice this year to 4.10% and in August projected 3.3% for 2026. Australia is now the clearest case of a tightening cycle that has not yet caught the data.

The UK added 0.4pp to 3.5% in September, inside the 3.1–3.6% 2026 range the Bank of England sketched across its April scenarios — though that vintage is now six months old and was explicitly built around uncertainty rather than a central path. The US rose 0.34pp to 3.74%, essentially at the Fed's own September 3.7% figure for 2026, with the funds rate at 3.50–3.75% and one cut still penciled in.

Asia is the counterweight. South Korea fell 0.21pp to 2.88% and Singapore eased 0.1pp to 2.2%. Japan's 2.2% and China's 0.9% both ticked up a tenth — not enough to call a move.

The IMF's current WEO vintage carries 2026 forecasts of 3.6% for the US, 3.3% for the UK, 3.1% for the Euro Area and 3.9% for Australia, with global inflation at 4.4% and broad upside revisions across advanced economies on energy pass-through. Three of those four country forecasts now sit below the latest actual print.

**Watch two things:** whether the Euro Area's gap to the ECB's 3.0% forecast forces a December revision, and whether Korea and Singapore's divergence from the Anglosphere holds through the next round of prints.
