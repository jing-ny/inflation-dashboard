# Inflation Newsletter — 2026-06-01

Global inflation held steady across major economies this period, but beneath the surface calm lies a brewing storm of central bank divergence and energy-shock aftereffects that's reshaping the 2026 outlook.

**The Big Picture: Stability Masks Underlying Tension**

Current readings show remarkable uniformity — the US at 3.8%, Euro Area at 3.0%, and most other major economies unchanged from prior months. Yet this snapshot belies the dramatic forecast revisions underway. The IMF's April WEO marked up global inflation projections sharply, with the US and Euro Area each seeing 0.7-0.8 percentage point upward revisions for 2026, while Australia and New Zealand faced full percentage-point increases.

**Policy Divergence Widens**

Central banks are splitting into distinct camps. The Reserve Bank of Australia delivered two consecutive hikes in early 2026, pushing rates to 4.10% as trimmed mean inflation threatens to peak at 3.7% — well above the 2-3% target band through early 2027. Brazil's central bank took an even more aggressive stance, lifting the Selic rate to 14.25% in March as inflation expectations remained stubbornly elevated.

Meanwhile, other major economies are holding steady or cutting. Mexico's Banxico reduced rates to 9.50% as inflation converged toward target, while Canada's BoC maintains a cautious 2.25% stance despite trade uncertainty from ongoing US tariff reviews.

**The China-Venezuela Extremes**

China's 1.0% March reading reinforces its disinflationary environment, with the People's Bank maintaining ultra-accommodative policy. The IMF projects just 1.2% inflation for 2026 — a stark contrast to Venezuela's hyperinflationary 618% that continues to distort regional comparisons despite post-crisis recovery efforts.

**What to Watch**

Three key divergences demand attention: Australia's aggressive tightening cycle versus the broader developed-world pause; Brazil's monetary hawks fighting persistent price pressures; and the growing gap between IMF projections and current central bank forecasts, particularly in energy-sensitive economies where the April shock may require further policy recalibration.
